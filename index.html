Quantum Loom

One quantum particle, simulated rather than faked — and the Fourier transform that makes it behave strangely.

Press and drag anywhere on the picture, then let go

 

What you are looking at: the chance of finding one quantum particle at each point. Bright means likely, dark means unlikely. This is a real simulation of the Schrödinger equation, recomputed on a 128 × 128 grid every frame — not a recorded animation and not a decorative effect.

What you can do: press where you want the particle and drag, then let go. It starts moving the way you flicked. A tap drops it almost still; a long, sharp flick gives it a definite shove — and because of the Fourier trade-off, a more definite momentum means a less definite position, so a hard flick produces a wider blob, not a tighter one. Try both and compare the momentum picture above.

Density is |ψ|², what a measurement of position would find. Phase is the direction the wave is waving, which you cannot measure directly — but where the colours churn fastest, the particle has momentum that way, which is why the phase view explains the spectrum next to it.

Edges of the box

Periodic means the grid wraps around, like a video game screen: leave the right edge, arrive at the left. Nothing is lost, so probability is conserved exactly. The walled box is a genuinely different physical system: the wall absorbs whatever touches it, and the readout below tells you how much of your particle has gone.

The product of the two spreads is the interesting one: quantum mechanics forbids it from going below 0.500 ħ (that number is ħ/2). It is computed from the live wavefunction every frame, never clipped to look well-behaved, so if it ever dipped below the bound that would be a bug in this page rather than a loophole in physics.

How this is simulated

One particle, mass 1, ħ = 1, in a flat 2-D box. The wavefunction is advanced with a spectral method: the Schrödinger equation is diagonal in Fourier space, so each step multiplies every wavelength by its exact phase and transforms back. That is why the total probability stays at 1.000 instead of quietly drifting, and why a standing wave really does stand perfectly still.

Runs entirely offline. No network, no storage, nothing collected.
